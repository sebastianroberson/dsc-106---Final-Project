What does great court vision look like?

An NBA passing case study

Team: Sebastian Roberson, Kylan Huynh, Kevin Pyo

A pass only counts as an assist if a teammate makes the shot. Throw a perfect pass to an open shooter who bricks it, and the box score records nothing.

So when we call someone a "great passer," are we measuring their vision, or their teammates' shooting? NBA player-tracking cameras log every pass, every shot it leads to, and who it went to. That data lets us look past the assist column.

1 Beyond the assist

A potential assist is any pass that leads directly to a shot, made or missed. Each dot below is one player. If teammates made every shot a player set up, the dot would sit on the 100% line. Dots far to the right but low create plenty of good looks for teammates who don't finish them.

Hover a dot for details, click it to follow that player through the page, or switch the axes to compare other passing stats.

2 Where the ball goes

Vision also means who you find. This network shows every pass between a team's rotation players over the whole season. Each circle is a player, sized by passes made per game, and each arrow is passes per game from one player to another. A creator becomes a hub that the offense runs through.

Hover a player to isolate their passes: orange = passes out, blue = passes in. Hover an arrow to see how often that pass led to an assist.

3 Coming next

Next we'll separate passing volume from passing quality, adjusting for role and teammates' shooting, to see which players create the most good looks per touch. The page will end with a "build your own playmaker" view where readers weight the metrics themselves and see how the rankings change.

Prototype write-up

What have we done so far?

We built a reproducible data pipeline in Python that uses the nba_api package to pull NBA player-tracking data for the 2025–26 regular season. It combines the league passing dashboard (passes made, potential assists, secondary assists and assist points created) with box-score and advanced stats, plus each rotation player's pass-by-pass breakdown to every teammate. With that data we built the first interactive view, a scatter plot of potential assists vs. assists. It has guide lines showing teammate conversion rates, switchable axes, a minutes filter, player search and a linked player card. We also built a second, linked view, a team passing network. It shows who passes to whom, follows the player selected in the scatter, and isolates a player's passes on hover. Finally, we drafted the story structure: assists are the standard measure of passing, but they depend on teammates making shots, therefore we use tracking data to measure the chances a passer creates.

What will be the most challenging part to design, and why?

The hardest part will be turning "court vision," a fuzzy idea about decision-making, into something we can show honestly. Tracking data records what happened after a pass, not the options a player saw or passed up. Even potential assists still depend on whether the teammate chose to shoot, so we have to be careful not to overstate what any one metric means. A second challenge is that the pass networks get cluttered fast. A 10-player rotation has up to 90 directed connections, so we'll need filtering, highlighting and annotation to keep the main pattern readable without hiding the data. Finally, linking the views into one guided story (scrollytelling that moves from the league-wide scatter to a single team's network to a single player) without losing the reader's freedom to explore is both a design and an engineering challenge.
